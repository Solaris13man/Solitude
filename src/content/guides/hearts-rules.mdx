Hearts is the classic trick-taking game of *avoidance*: unlike most card games, you are
trying **not** to win the wrong tricks. Every heart costs you a point, the Queen of
Spades costs thirteen, and the player with the **lowest** score when someone hits
100 is the winner. This page is a complete reference to how Hearts is played. If you'd rather
learn by doing, you can [play Hearts](/hearts/) right now against three computer
opponents.

## What is Hearts?

Hearts is a four-player game played with a standard 52-card deck. There are no partnerships —
it is every player for themselves. Aces are high and there is no trump suit. The goal is to dodge
points across a series of hands, and the game ends when any player reaches the target score
(100 on CardHearth). At that moment, the player with the **fewest** points wins.

## The deal

All 52 cards are dealt out evenly, so each of the four players receives **13 cards**.
Nothing is left in a stock — every card is in someone's hand. You sort your hand, and then,
before a single card is played, comes the pass.

## The pass

At the start of most hands you choose **three cards** to pass face down to another
player, and you receive three from someone else. The direction **rotates** hand by
hand:

- **Hand 1:** pass left.
- **Hand 2:** pass right.
- **Hand 3:** pass across (to the player opposite you).
- **Hand 4:** no pass — you keep your hand (the "hold" hand).

Then the cycle repeats. You pick your three cards and pass them *before* you see what is
coming back, so the pass is your first real decision: it is your chance to unload dangerous cards
like the Queen of Spades, the Ace and King of Spades, or a long run of high hearts.

## The two of clubs leads

The player holding the **two of clubs** must lead it to begin the first trick. Play
then goes clockwise. Each player in turn plays one card, and you **must follow the led
suit** if you hold a card in it. If you are void in the led suit, you may play any card —
and that is exactly how you get rid of hearts and the Queen of Spades.

## Winning a trick

The highest card of the **led suit** wins the trick — there is no trump, so off-suit
cards (the ones you throw when you can't follow) can never win. Whoever wins the trick collects
those four cards face down and leads the next trick. The cards you win don't help you; they are
where your points come from.

## The no-points-on-the-first-trick rule

You may **not** play any point card — no heart, and not the Queen of Spades — on the
very first trick of a hand, unless your hand is somehow nothing but points. This keeps the
opening trick safe and stops anyone dumping the Queen immediately.

## Breaking hearts

You cannot **lead** a heart until hearts have been "broken". Hearts break the first
time someone, unable to follow the led suit, discards a heart onto a trick. By default the
**Queen of Spades** breaks hearts too — a common house rule — and you can turn that
off in **Settings** if you prefer that only a heart breaks. Until hearts are broken,
they can only be played when you are following suit or sloughing on another suit — never as the
card that opens a trick.

## Scoring

At the end of each hand, players count the point cards they took:

- Each **heart** is worth **1 point** (thirteen hearts = 13 points).
- The **Queen of Spades** is worth **13 points** on her own.
- Every other card is worth nothing.

So there are **26 points** in play every hand. Points are bad: you want to take as
few as possible. Scores accumulate across hands, and the game ends when someone reaches
**100**. The player with the lowest total then wins.

## Shooting the moon

There is one spectacular exception. If a single player takes **all 26 points** in a
hand — every heart and the Queen of Spades — they have **shot the moon**. Rather
than scoring 26, that player scores **zero** and each of the other three players is
hit with **26 points**. It is a high-risk, high-reward gambit: miss by a single
heart and you simply collect all those points yourself.

## How you win

Hands continue until a player's running total reaches 100 or more. The game then stops and the
**lowest** score wins. Because a single bad hand — or one successful moon shot
against you — can swing 26 points, Hearts stays tense right to the end.
