Mahjong Solitaire is a **single-player tile-matching puzzle**. You take a pile
of tiles stacked into a layered pattern and dismantle it by removing matching pairs, one pair
at a time, until nothing is left. It goes by several names — *Mahjong Tiles*,
*Shanghai*, *Taipei*, or just "Mahjong" on a phone or computer — but it is
always the same calm, methodical solo game. If you'd rather learn by doing, you can
[play a board right now](/mahjong/) and refer back here as questions come up.

## Mahjong Solitaire is not the four-player game Mahjong

This is the single most common point of confusion, so it's worth stating plainly. Traditional
**Mahjong** is a social game for four players, built around drawing and
discarding tiles to complete a scoring hand — closer in spirit to rummy or gin. Mahjong
*Solitaire* borrows the same beautiful set of 144 tiles, but that's where the
resemblance ends: there are no opponents, no hands, no discards and no draws. The goal and
the rules are entirely different. Here you simply find pairs and clear the table.

## The tiles

A full set has **144 tiles**, drawn from a few families:

- **Three suits** — circles (dots), bamboo, and characters — each numbered 1
  through 9, with four copies of every number.
- **Honor tiles** — the four winds and the three dragons — again with four
  copies each.
- **Bonus tiles** — four flowers and four seasons.

For matching, most tiles need an **exact identical twin**: a circle 5 pairs only
with another circle 5, the east wind only with another east wind, and so on. The bonus tiles
are the one exception — **any flower matches any other flower**, and
**any season matches any other season**. That small rule is what lets the bonus
tiles come off even though no two of them are truly identical.

## The layout

The tiles aren't laid flat in a grid. They're stacked into a **multi-layer 3D
pattern**, with upper tiles resting partly on top of the ones below. The classic shape
is the five-layer [**Turtle**](/mahjong-turtle/) (sometimes called the
Dragon), but it's only one of several formations — CardHearth also deals a quick Cross, a
pointed Diamond, a stepped Ziggurat, a twin-towered Bridge, a winged Butterfly, a walled
Fortress, and a moated Arena. The layout is what gives the game its puzzle: tiles bury one
another, so the order you remove them in matters.

## The objective

The goal is simple to state: **remove every tile**. You do that by selecting two
matching **free** tiles to take them off the board, then repeating until the
whole layout is clear. With 144 tiles that's 72 pairs to find. Clear them all and you've won.

## What makes a tile "free"

You can't match just any two tiles — only ones that are currently **free**. A
tile is free when **both** of these are true:

- **Nothing rests on top of it** — no tile from a higher layer covers it.
- **At least one long side is open** — its left edge or its right edge has no
  tile touching it.

A tile that's buried, or hemmed in by neighbours on both the left and right, is locked. As you
clear pairs, locked tiles become free, which is what keeps the puzzle moving.

## How you win — and how you get stuck

You **win** by clearing every tile from the board. You can also get
**stuck**: if you reach a point where no two free tiles match, there are no legal
moves left and the layout can't be finished from that position. Importantly, this isn't always
bad luck. **Every CardHearth deal is built to be solvable** — deals are
assembled by placing pairs in reverse play order, which guarantees at least one path to a
clear board. But a wrong removal *order* can still strand you (for instance, if two
identical tiles end up stacked directly on top of each other). That's exactly what
**undo** is for: step back a few pairs, choose differently, and the winning path
is always there. The hint button will surface a matching free pair whenever one exists.
