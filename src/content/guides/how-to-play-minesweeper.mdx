Minesweeper looks intimidating — a grid full of blank squares, a hidden field of mines — but the rules are simple and you can learn them in a couple of minutes. This guide covers the basics from the ground up: what the board is, what the numbers mean, how to reveal and flag squares, and the handful of deductions that let you clear a board safely. The fastest way to follow along is to [open a game](/minesweeper/) and read this alongside it.

## What is Minesweeper?

Minesweeper is a single-player logic puzzle. A grid of squares hides a fixed number of mines, and your job is to **reveal every safe square without detonating a single mine**. You are never asked to find the mines directly — you find the safe squares, and the mines are simply everything you leave untouched. It is pure deduction: the board gives you clues, and you work out where it is and isn't safe to click.

## The board and the objective

The board is a rectangular grid of cells. Some unknown subset of those cells contain mines; the rest are safe. On CardHearth, Easy is 9×9 with 10 mines, Medium is 16×16 with 40 mines, and [Expert](/minesweeper-expert/) is the classic 30×16 board with 99 mines. Whatever the size, the goal is identical: **clear every cell that is not a mine**. Do that and you win — you never need to "click" the mines themselves.

## What the numbers mean

This is the heart of the game. When you reveal a safe cell, it shows a **number from 1 to 8**, and that number tells you exactly how many of its eight neighbouring cells (the ones touching it horizontally, vertically, and diagonally) contain mines. A 1 means one of those eight neighbours is a mine; a 3 means three of them are.

A revealed cell that is completely blank is a **0** — none of its neighbours are mines. Because a 0 is surrounded entirely by safe cells, the game automatically opens all of those neighbours for you, and if any of *them* are also 0s, it keeps cascading outward. That is why a single click in an open area can suddenly clear a large region of the board at once.

## Controls: revealing and flagging

There are two actions. **Revealing** a cell (a normal click or tap) opens it, showing either a number, a blank, or — if you guessed wrong — a mine. **Flagging** a cell marks it as a suspected mine so you remember not to click it. On CardHearth you flag with a right-click, a long-press on touch screens, or by switching on Flag mode (the ⚑ button or the F key).

Flags are entirely optional. They do not affect whether you win — they are just a memory aid, and a flag does not even have to be correct. The mine counter in the display simply shows the mine total minus the flags you have placed, to help you keep track.

## Two things CardHearth does for you

Two CardHearth features make learning much friendlier than the classic version:

- **The first click is always safe.** Mines are placed only *after* your first reveal, and the game deliberately keeps them away from the cell you clicked and its neighbours. So your opening move can never blow up, and it always opens a starting area to read from.
- **You can undo a misclick.** Hitting a mine no longer permanently ends the game — you can take the move back and carry on. Purists can ignore this; beginners can lean on it to learn from mistakes without starting over.

## Getting started: your first deductions

After the first click opens an area, work along the edge of the revealed region where the numbers sit next to hidden cells. A few simple rules will carry you a long way:

- **Click in the open.** If you have a large blank area, clicking inside it reveals lots of squares at once and gives you more numbers to reason from.
- **Satisfied numbers make the rest safe.** If a number already touches as many flags (known mines) as its value, then all of its *other* hidden neighbours must be safe — you can reveal them.
- **Forced mines.** If a number's count exactly equals the number of unknown cells still touching it, then every one of those unknown cells is a mine. A 1 touching a single hidden square means that square is the mine.

## Winning and losing

You **win** the instant every safe cell is revealed — you do not have to flag the mines, and CardHearth tracks how many mines remain for you as you go. You **lose** by revealing a mine (though, again, undo means a slip need not be the end). Most boards can be solved by logic alone; only occasionally will a position leave you with a genuine guess. When that happens, and when you want to recognise recurring shapes like the **1-2-1** pattern or weigh up the odds of a guess, head to our [Minesweeper strategy guide](/guides/minesweeper-strategy/), which covers deduction patterns and probability in depth.
