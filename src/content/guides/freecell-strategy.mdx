FreeCell is the thinking player's solitaire. Unlike Klondike, **every card is dealt
face-up from the start**, so there is no hidden information and no luck of the draw —
the entire game sits in front of you. That single difference changes everything: winning is
not about hoping the next card helps, it's about *planning*. And the planning pays off,
because almost every deal can be won. This guide explains the principles that turn a visible
board into a solved one, and *why* each one matters.

## The goal, in one sentence

Build the four **foundations** upward by suit, Ace to King. You do that by
arranging the eight **tableau** columns into descending runs of alternating
colour (a red Eight on a black Nine), using the four **free cells** — each of
which holds exactly one card — as temporary storage. If the rules are new, the quickest way to
learn is to [play a hand](/freecell/) with hints on.

## The one mechanic that decides everything

You can only ever move **one card at a time**. The reason you appear to move whole
runs is that free cells and empty columns let the game shuffle the cards for you. The maximum
number you can shift in a single "supermove" is:

**(1 + empty free cells) × 2<sup>(empty tableau columns)</sup>**

With four free cells open and no empty columns, that's five cards. Free up a single column and
it doubles to ten. In other words, **your empty free cells and empty columns are your
mobility** — they are the resource the whole game is fought over. Guarding them, and
creating more of them, is the heart of FreeCell strategy.

## Five principles that win games

### 1. Keep your free cells empty

Treat parking a card in a free cell as **borrowing** — a debt you must have a
plan to repay. Every cell you fill cuts your supermove size and your options. Four occupied
cells with no way to unload them is the single most common way to lose a winnable deal. Use a
cell only when you can see how the card comes back out.

### 2. Work to empty a column early

An empty column is worth far more than an empty free cell, because it *multiplies* how
much you can move rather than just adding one. Emptying even one column early transforms the
board, so prioritise consolidating short piles to open a space. Just don't fill a hard-won
empty column with a stray card unless it advances your plan.

### 3. Plan several moves ahead before committing

Because the whole deal is visible, you can — and should — read sequences out in your head
before touching anything. Trace where a card will go, what it frees, and how you'll recover
any cells you spend, all the way to the foundation. The best FreeCell players move slowly and
deliberately; the board isn't going anywhere.

### 4. Send low cards up — but not the ones you still need

Getting Aces and Twos to the foundations frees space and unclogs the tableau. But don't strip
the board of cards you still need to *receive* runs. A Five you bank too early might be
exactly the landing spot a black Four and red Three were waiting for. Promote a card when it
no longer has work to do on the tableau, not the instant it becomes legal.

### 5. Hunt for buried aces and twos — and plan the dig

Aces and Twos stuck near the bottom of a long column block everything above their suit. Spot
them at the very start and plan the excavation: which cells and empty columns you'll spend to
reach them, and how you'll recover afterwards. Digging out a deep Ace early often unlocks the
entire deal.

## Common mistakes that lose games

- **Filling all four free cells with no exit plan** — the number-one win-killer.
- **Burying an ace** under a long column instead of digging it out early.
- **Sending cards to the foundation too eagerly** when they were still needed on
  the tableau.
- **Never creating an empty column**, leaving your supermoves tiny all game.
- **Moving on autopilot** — with everything visible, a few seconds of planning
  beats a dozen reflex moves.
