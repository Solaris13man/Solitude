Sudoku looks intimidating — a grid full of numbers — but it is one of the easiest puzzles to learn and one of the most satisfying to master. There is only a single rule, you never do any arithmetic, and every puzzle can be solved by reasoning alone. This guide takes you from a blank grid to confidently placing your first numbers. When you want to go further, our [advanced techniques guide](/guides/sudoku-solving-techniques/) picks up where this one leaves off.

## What is Sudoku?

Sudoku is a logic puzzle played on a **9&times;9 grid** — eighty-one squares in all. Some squares already contain numbers (the **clues**, or givens); the rest are blank, and your job is to fill them in. The digits 1 through 9 are used purely as nine different symbols, so **no math is needed**: you are not adding anything, just working out where each digit belongs. If you would rather see it in motion than read about it, you can [play a puzzle now](/sudoku/) and follow along.

## The one rule

Everything in Sudoku comes from a single rule: **fill the grid so that every row, every column, and every 3&times;3 box contains the digits 1 to 9 exactly once.** That is the whole game. To apply it, you need to picture the three kinds of group:

- **Rows** — the nine horizontal lines running left to right. Each row holds nine squares and must contain each digit 1&ndash;9 once.
- **Columns** — the nine vertical lines running top to bottom. Same deal: every digit once, no repeats.
- **Boxes** — the grid is divided by heavier lines into nine 3&times;3 blocks. Each box must also contain each digit exactly once.

Because of this, a digit can never repeat within the same row, column or box. Every square sits at the crossing of one row, one column and one box, so it must satisfy all three at the same time — and that overlap is exactly what lets you deduce the answer.

## Getting started

Begin by reading the clues already on the board — they are the foundation everything else is built on. Then look for squares whose value is **forced**: a square where only one digit can legally go, because every other digit already appears in its row, its column or its box. Filling one square often forces the next, and the puzzle unravels from there. New players do well to start on an [easy puzzle](/sudoku-easy/), where plenty of clues make these forced squares easy to spot.

## The two beginner techniques

Almost every easy puzzle can be finished with just two ideas, both found by **scanning** — sweeping your eye along rows, columns and boxes to see what is missing.

### Naked single

A **naked single** is a square that has only one possible digit left. Look at an empty square and check its row, column and box. If eight of the nine digits already appear somewhere in those three groups, the ninth digit is the only one that fits — so it must go there. The answer is &ldquo;naked&rdquo; because there is nothing to hide behind.

### Hidden single

A **hidden single** works the other way around. Pick a digit and a single group — say, a box — and ask where that digit can possibly go inside it. If every empty square but one is blocked (because the digit already sits in that square&rsquo;s row or column), then the one remaining square must take the digit, even if that square could in theory hold other numbers too. Scanning one digit at a time across all nine boxes is the fastest way to find these.

## Pencil marks and candidates

When scanning stops turning up easy placements, switch to **pencil marks** (also called notes or candidates). For each empty square you write small the digits that could still legally go there. With the candidates laid out, naked singles jump out as squares with only one note, and you can spot patterns you would miss in your head. On CardHearth, toggle **Notes** (the &#10070; button, or press **P**) and the board keeps your candidates tidy — placing a real digit automatically erases that candidate from every square it rules out.

## Tips for beginners

- **Start with the most-filled groups.** The row, column or box with the fewest blanks is the easiest place to find a forced square.
- **Scan one digit at a time.** Pick a digit that already appears often and hunt for hidden singles across the boxes.
- **Pencil in candidates once scanning stalls** — and keep them current as you place digits.
- **Never guess.** If nothing is obvious, the deduction is just hiding; re-check your notes rather than trying a number to &ldquo;see what happens.&rdquo;
- **Use undo freely.** On CardHearth undo is unlimited, so exploring is safe.

Once these basics feel natural and you move on to Hard and Expert puzzles, you will meet longer deduction chains that need a few more tools — pairs, pointing, X-Wings and the like. Our [Sudoku solving techniques guide](/guides/sudoku-solving-techniques/) walks through each of them in order.
