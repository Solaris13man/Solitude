Spider Solitaire looks forgiving — you can drop almost any card onto almost any other — but
that freedom is a trap. The cards that *move together* and the runs that actually
*finish* are far more restrictive than they first appear. Skilled players win Spider by
thinking about suits and sequencing from the very first move, not by stacking whatever fits.
This guide explains the principles that win games and, just as importantly, *why* they
work. If the rules are new, the quickest way to learn is to
[play a hand](/spider/) with hints turned on.

## The goal, in one sentence

Build descending runs from **King down to Ace, all in a single suit**. The moment
a complete same-suit King-to-Ace run forms in a column, it is removed automatically from the
board. Clear all eight of those suit-runs — emptying the ten columns in the process — and
you win. That single rule drives everything else: only same-suit sequences ever complete, so
only same-suit building makes lasting progress.

## Five principles that win games

### 1. Build in suit whenever you can

You are allowed to place a card on any card one rank higher, regardless of suit — a black Nine
on a red Ten is legal. But that off-suit stack is **temporary**. Only a same-suit
run moves as a single unit, and only a same-suit run completes and clears. So treat off-suit
placements as scaffolding you will dismantle later, and prefer the in-suit move every time it
is available, even if it looks less tidy. The whole game is really about converting messy
off-suit piles into clean same-suit runs.

### 2. Turn over face-down cards as a priority

Every hidden card you expose is new information and a new option. When two moves are otherwise
equal, choose the one that flips a face-down card. Columns you can never dig into are dead
weight for the rest of the game, so dig into them early while you still have the freedom to
maneuver.

### 3. Treat empty columns as your most valuable asset

An empty column is the single strongest resource in Spider. It can hold *any* card or
run, which means it doubles as a workspace: you can park an off-suit card there, pull a buried
same-suit run out from underneath it, and reassemble runs in the right order. Work hard to
create at least one empty column, and think twice before refilling it — an empty column you
keep open is worth more than almost any single move you could spend it on.

### 4. Make every useful move before you deal

When you deal a new row from the stock, a fresh card lands on top of **every**
column at once, burying whatever was there. A deal made too early can cover a run you were
about to complete. So squeeze out all the in-suit consolidation, all the face-down flips, and
all the tidying you can *before* you deal. One important constraint: you cannot deal at
all while any column is empty, so plan to fill gaps deliberately right before a deal rather
than being forced into a bad move.

### 5. Anchor long runs with your Kings

Because runs build downward from the King, Kings are natural anchors. Keep them positioned
where a same-suit run can grow beneath them, and try to consolidate matching suits toward those
Kings. A King sitting in a clear column with its suit-mates lining up below it is the picture of
a column about to clear.

## Choosing your difficulty

Spider scales sharply with the number of suits. **1-suit** is a gentle
introduction — every card is the same suit, so any descending sequence both moves and completes,
and it is ideal for learning the flow of dealing and clearing. **2-suit** is the
popular middle ground and where most players spend their time: it demands genuine in-suit
decisions without overwhelming you. **4-suit** is a serious challenge that punishes
every careless off-suit tangle. CardHearth offers both
[2-suit Spider](/spider-2-suits/) and [4-suit Spider](/spider-4-suits/),
each with **unlimited undo and hints** so you can experiment, see the consequences
of a line, and learn faster than you would grinding blind.

## Common mistakes that lose games

- **Dealing from the stock too early** — the number-one win-killer, because a
  premature deal buries progress under ten fresh cards.
- **Creating off-suit tangles** you then can't unwind, leaving runs that refuse
  to move as a unit.
- **Filling or emptying columns carelessly**, spending your most powerful
  resource on a move that doesn't advance a suit-run.
- **Ignoring face-down cards** in favor of tidy-looking sideways shuffles.
- **Forgetting undo exists** — on CardHearth, undo is unlimited, so probe a line
  and take it back if it dead-ends.

## Practise the right way

Use *Hint* when you are genuinely stuck to see which in-suit consolidation you missed,
and lean on unlimited *Undo* to test whether a deal is safe before you commit to it.
Replaying the same kind of position until the in-suit and empty-column instincts feel automatic
is far more effective than rushing through random deals.
