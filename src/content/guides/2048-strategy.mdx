2048 looks like a game of luck — tiles spawn at random, the board fills up, and one bad swipe ends a run that felt like it was going well. But the players who hit 2048 again and again aren't luckier; they follow a handful of rules that turn the random spawns into something predictable. This guide explains that method and, just as importantly, *why* each rule works. The fastest way to follow along is to [play a round](/2048/) while you read.

## How 2048 works

Every move, you slide **all** the tiles in one of four directions — up, down, left or right. When two tiles with the same number collide, they **merge** into a single tile worth their sum: two 2s become a 4, two 4s become an 8, and so on up the chain. After each move a new tile (usually a 2, occasionally a 4) **spawns** on a random empty square. You win when you build the **2048** tile — and you can keep going for higher tiles afterwards. You lose only when the board is completely full and no merge is possible in any direction.

## The strategy that wins games

### 1. Pick a corner and never let your biggest tile leave it

This is the single most important habit. Choose one corner — most strong players use **bottom-right or bottom-left** — and treat it as the permanent home for your largest tile. Why a corner? A tile in the middle of the board can be pushed in four directions and constantly gets separated from the tiles it needs to merge with. A tile in a corner only moves when you swipe toward the two walls it's already touching, so it stays put while everything else organises around it.

### 2. Build a monotonic chain — the "snake"

Keep your tiles in **decreasing order** along the row or column that leads into your anchor corner. Imagine a snake winding back and forth: the biggest tile in the corner, the next-biggest beside it, then the next, all the way down to your small new tiles. When the values descend in order, a merge at one end **cascades** — your 256 merges into your 512, freeing the space that lets the next pair combine, and the whole row collapses upward into the big tile. A jumbled board can't do that.

### 3. Favour two directions and avoid the move that frees your anchor

If your anchor is in the bottom-right, build using mostly **down and right**. Those two swipes keep pressing everything toward the corner and maintaining the snake. The move you must guard against is the opposite one — never swipe **up** when your anchor sits on the bottom unless you have genuinely no other legal move. A single careless "up" can rip your largest tile out of its corner and undo the whole structure in one swipe.

### 4. Keep your anchor row full

Try to keep the bottom row (or whichever row holds your anchor) **completely filled**. An empty square in that row is an invitation for a stray 2 to spawn *underneath* your big tiles, wedging a useless small value into the structure that you then have to dig out. A full anchor row also means a downward swipe doesn't disturb it — your foundation stays locked while you work the rows above.

### 5. Plan merges before the board gets tight

Don't swipe on autopilot. Look at the board and think about which row is about to collapse into the next size up, and which swipe sets that up without breaking the snake. The time to think is *before* you're down to one or two empty squares — once the board is crowded, your choices vanish and you're forced into the move you've been avoiding. A little planning early keeps you out of that corner (the bad kind).

## Common mistakes that end runs

- **Swiping all four directions at random** — the fastest way to scatter your tiles and lose the snake.
- **Moving the anchor out of its corner** — one stray "up" can cost you the whole game.
- **Chasing small merges in the wrong direction**, pulling tiles away from the corner just to combine a couple of 4s.
- **Filling the board with no plan**, leaving mismatched tiles that can never merge.
- **Forgetting undo exists** — on CardHearth you can take back a bad swipe, so a single mistake doesn't have to end the run.

## Practise the right way

CardHearth gives every board a number, so you can **replay the exact same start** to test a different line or share it with a friend to see who climbs higher. Use *Undo* to take back a swipe that broke your snake and learn from it — that's how the corner-and-snake method becomes second nature far faster than grinding random boards.
