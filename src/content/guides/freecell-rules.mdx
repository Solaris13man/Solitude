FreeCell is the open-information solitaire: there is nothing hidden, no luck of the draw, and
nearly every deal can be won. That makes the rules easy to state and the game hard to put
down. This page is a complete reference to how FreeCell is played — the layout, the deal, the
legal moves, and how you win. If you'd rather learn by doing, you can
[play FreeCell](/freecell/) straight away with unlimited undo and hints.

## What is FreeCell?

FreeCell is a solitaire game played with a single standard 52-card deck. Every card is dealt
**face up**, so you can see the entire deal from the first move — there is no
stock to draw from and nothing concealed. The table has three kinds of pile: four
**free cells** (single-card holding spaces), four **foundations**
(where you build winning piles), and eight **tableau columns**, also called
cascades, where the cards start out. Your job is to use the free cells and the tableau to
unpack the deal and feed every card onto the foundations.

## The setup

All 52 cards are dealt face up across the **eight tableau columns**. The deal is
not even: the cards are dealt one at a time across the row, so the
**first four columns get seven cards each** and the
**last four columns get six cards each**. That is 4&nbsp;&times;&nbsp;7 plus
4&nbsp;&times;&nbsp;6 = 28 + 24 = **52 cards**, the whole deck. The
**four free cells** and the **four foundations** all start empty.
Because nothing is face down, the position you see at the start is the position you have to
solve — no surprises arrive later.

## The objective

You win by building all four **foundations** up by suit, each running from
**Ace to King**. Each foundation takes one suit: you start it with that suit's
Ace, then add the Two, the Three, and so on up to the King. When all four foundations hold a
complete Ace-to-King suit — 13 cards each, 52 in total — the game is won.

## Legal moves

FreeCell has a small, strict set of rules for where a card may go. Everything else follows
from these.

- **Tableau builds down in alternating colours.** You may place a card on a
  tableau column only if it is one rank lower than the column's top card and the
  *opposite* colour — a red Six on a black Seven, a black Four on a red Five, and so
  on.
- **A free cell holds exactly one card.** You may move any single card into an
  empty free cell, and move it back out later when it fits somewhere. A free cell that is
  already occupied is unavailable until you empty it.
- **Foundations build up by suit from the Ace.** A card goes to a foundation only
  if it is the Ace of an empty foundation, or the next rank up in the same suit as the
  foundation's top card.
- **An empty column accepts any card.** When a tableau column is cleared, any
  single card — or the lead of a run you can relay — may be placed there.

Officially, every move is a **single card** moving between a tableau column, a
free cell, and a foundation. There is no separate rule for moving a group.

## The supermove: moving several cards at once

Most FreeCell apps, CardHearth included, let you drag a whole descending, alternating-colour
run in one gesture. This is the **supermove**, and it is purely a convenience: it
automates a series of legal single moves that shuffle cards through your empty free cells and
empty columns and back. Because of that, the number of cards you can move at once is limited
by how much temporary space you have. The rule is:

**(1 + empty free cells) &times; 2<sup>(empty columns)</sup>**

In words: take one, add the number of empty free cells, then double the result once for each
empty column. With all four cells free and no empty columns you can move five cards
(1 + 4 = 5). Free up one column and that doubles to ten; a second empty column would double it
again to twenty. One important caveat: when you are moving a run *onto* an empty column,
that destination column does not count toward the doubling, because it is where the run is
going rather than a relay space.

## How you win

The game is won the moment every card sits on a foundation — all four suits built up cleanly
from Ace to King. Free cells and tableau columns end up empty. Once the remaining position is
provably safe to finish, an auto-complete option appears so you don't have to click every last
card up by hand.

## Are FreeCell deals always solvable?

Almost always. In the famous original collection of 32,000 numbered FreeCell deals, exactly
one — **deal&nbsp;#11982** — has been proven impossible to win. Every other deal
in that set is solvable with perfect play, and across random deals generally the solvable rate
is well above 99%. That is why FreeCell is treated as a puzzle of skill: if you lose, the deal
was almost certainly winnable, and unlimited undo lets you back up and find the line you
missed.
