Minesweeper looks like a game of nerve, but it's really a logic puzzle. Almost every move you
make can be *deduced* — the numbers on the board tell you exactly where the mines are
if you read them carefully. This guide shows you how to turn that wall of digits into a clear
map of safe squares, so you spend far less time guessing and far more time reasoning. If the
rules are new to you, the quickest way to learn is to [play a board](/minesweeper/)
and follow along.

## The goal, in one sentence

Reveal every **safe** square without detonating a mine. Each number you uncover
tells you **how many mines touch that square** across its eight surrounding
neighbours (including diagonals). You don't have to flag anything to win — flagging is just a
bookkeeping tool to mark squares you've proven are mines — but used well it makes the harder
deductions far easier to see.

## Two things CardHearth does differently

Before the tactics, two rules on CardHearth change how you can play, and you should lean on
both:

- **The first click is always safe.** Mines are placed *after* your
  opening click, and the board is arranged so neither the square you clicked nor any of its
  neighbours holds a mine. So your first click never loses — and it cascades open a chunk of
  empty board, handing you numbers to start reasoning from. Click in the open, near the
  middle, to crack the board open fast.
- **You can undo a misclick.** A fatal click no longer ends the game forever on
  CardHearth — you can undo it and continue. That lets you back out of a careless slip or test
  a line and take it back. The aim is still to win by logic, but you're never one stray click
  from starting over.

## Reading the numbers: the core deductions

### 1. The basic count rule

Every number is a tiny equation: it equals the number of mines among its unknown neighbours.
Two situations let you act immediately:

- **Satisfied number → the rest are safe.** If a number already touches as many
  flagged mines as its value (a "2" with two flags beside it), every *other* covered
  neighbour is guaranteed safe. Open them all.
- **Forced number → the rest are mines.** If a number's count of remaining
  *unknown* neighbours exactly equals its remaining value (a "3" with exactly three
  covered squares left), then all of those squares are mines. Flag them.

Most of any board is solved by applying just these two rules over and over, square by square.

### 2. Subtraction and overlap logic

When the basic rule stalls, look at **two adjacent numbers that share squares**.
Suppose a "1" and a "2" sit side by side and share two covered cells, but the "2" also touches
one extra covered cell the "1" doesn't. The "1" says exactly one mine sits in the shared pair.
Subtract that from the "2": of its remaining mines, after accounting for the shared one, that
extra cell *must* be a mine. This "subtraction" trick — taking what one number proves
and feeding it into its neighbour — unlocks positions the count rule alone can't.

### 3. The 1-2-1 and 1-2-2-1 patterns

Some shapes recur so often they're worth memorising. Along an edge or wall, a row reading
**1-2-1** against a line of covered squares forces the mines to sit under the
*two outer* 1s, with the middle square (under the 2) safe. The longer
**1-2-2-1** pattern forces mines under the *two middle* squares, leaving
the outer two safe. Spotting these on sight saves you re-deriving the same subtraction every
time — they're the most common "free" moves on a frontier.

### 4. Chording for speed

Once a number has all its mines correctly flagged, you can **chord** it: open all
its remaining neighbours in a single action instead of clicking each one. This is purely a
speed tool, but on a board where you've flagged carefully it clears huge regions instantly.
The catch: chord only on numbers whose flags you've truly *proven* — a wrong flag turns
a chord into a detonation.

## Working the board: a game plan

**Open it up first.** Click in the open to trigger a cascade and expose a big
field of numbers — the more numbers you have, the more you can deduce. Then
**work the frontier**: the border where revealed numbers meet covered squares is
where every deduction lives. Walk along it applying the count rule, then the subtraction rule,
then pattern-matching, and you'll peel the board open in waves.

**When you're genuinely forced to guess**, do it intelligently. Estimate the mine
probability for each candidate square and pick the **lowest**. Squares on edges
and especially in **corners** touch fewer neighbours, so they're often safer
bets, and a good guess is one that *opens new information* rather than a dead cell in a
corner you'll never read again. Late in the game, **count**: compare the mine
counter against the number of covered squares remaining — if eight covered squares hold two
mines, every individual square is only 25% likely to be a mine, and sometimes the global count
proves a square safe outright.

## Common mistakes that lose games

- **Flagging by guess instead of deduction.** A flag you placed on a hunch
  poisons every number around it and leads to bad chords. Only flag what you've proven.
- **Ignoring the mine counter late-game.** The global mine count is a free clue
  — players who never glance at it miss forced moves that the local numbers alone can't show.
- **Guessing when a logical safe move exists.** The most common loss isn't bad
  luck; it's clicking a "coin-flip" square while an undisturbed corner of the frontier still
  held a provable safe square. Exhaust the logic first.
