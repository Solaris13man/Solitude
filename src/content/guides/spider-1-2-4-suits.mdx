Spider Solitaire greets you with a choice that trips up a lot of newcomers: do you want to play
with **one suit, two suits, or four suits**? It sounds like three different games,
but it isn't. The rules are identical in every version — the suit count is simply the
**difficulty dial**. Turn it up and the same game gets dramatically harder. This
guide explains what changes (and what doesn't), then helps you decide which version to
[play](/spider/) today.

## The one rule that never changes

Spider is dealt from **two full decks** — 104 cards. You arrange cards into
descending sequences in the ten tableau columns, and your goal is always the same: build a
complete **King-to-Ace run in a single suit**. When you do, that run is removed
from the table. Assemble all eight same-suit runs and you've won. That goal is fixed no matter
how many suits are in play. What the suit count changes is how hard those same-suit runs are to
put together — because only a genuine same-suit sequence can be picked up and moved as one
unit, or completed and cleared.

## One suit: the gentlest version

In one-suit Spider every card is a spade. That single fact transforms the game. Because there's
only one suit, **any** descending sequence is automatically a same-suit run — which
means it's always movable as a unit and always eligible to be completed and removed. You never
have to worry about colours or matching suits; if the numbers go down in order, the cards move
together. The result is the **highest win rate** of the three and the cleanest way
to learn how Spider flows: dealing rows, opening columns, and clearing runs.

## Two suits: the popular middle ground

Two-suit Spider — usually spades and hearts — is where most regular players settle. Now you have
to keep **two suits untangled**. You can still stack a card on any card one rank
higher regardless of suit, but only a same-suit sequence moves as a block, so building
**in-suit** genuinely matters. Mix the suits carelessly and you'll bury cards you
needed; plan ahead and there's real room to recover. It's a meaningful step up from one suit
without being punishing, which is exactly why it's the default challenge for so many people.
CardHearth has a dedicated [2-suit Spider](/spider-2-suits/) page if you want to jump
straight in.

## Four suits: the full challenge

Four-suit Spider uses all four suits across both decks, and it is a different beast. Here, only
a **true same-suit run** can be moved as a unit or completed — so off-suit stacking
is constant and unavoidable. You'll spend much of the game parking cards on near-matches just to
free up space, then patiently re-sorting them by suit. Win rates are low even for strong
players, and a single misordered card can lock up a column for good. It's the version
experienced players reach for when they want the game to fight back. Our
[4-suit Spider](/spider-4-suits/) page has it ready when you are.

## Which should you play?

Think of it as a progression rather than a one-time pick:

- **One suit → Easy → New players.** Learn the rhythm of the game with the cards
  working with you, not against you.
- **Two suits → Medium → Most players.** The natural default once one suit feels
  automatic — challenging but recoverable.
- **Four suits → Hard → Experienced players.** The full test, best attempted once
  two-suit games feel comfortable.

In short: start at **one suit** to learn the flow, make **two suits**
your everyday challenge, and try **four suits** once two-suit deals stop giving you
trouble. CardHearth offers the two-suit and four-suit variants on their own pages, and every
deal comes with **unlimited undo and hints** — the best way to practise a harder
suit count without grinding through losses.
