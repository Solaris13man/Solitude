Mahjong Solitaire is a tile-matching puzzle, not the four-player Mahjong game — you play it
alone, against a layout of stacked tiles. It looks relaxing, and it is, but there is real
skill underneath. The difference between players who clear the board and players who get stuck
is almost never luck. It is the order in which they remove tiles. This guide explains how to
read the board and pick the right pairs. If the game is new to you, the quickest way to learn
is to [play a hand](/mahjong/) with hints turned on.

## The goal, in one sentence

Remove every tile from the layout by matching pairs of identical tiles that are
**free**. A tile is free when it has **no tile on top of it** and at
least one of its **left or right sides is open**. Match two free, identical tiles
and both vanish; clear the entire layout and you win. There are **four copies** of
every tile, so each kind is removed as two separate pairs.

Two groups behave differently: the four **flower** tiles all match each other, and
the four **season** tiles all match each other — you do not need an exact twin for
these. Flowers and seasons never mix, though. Every other tile demands its identical partner.

## Why CardHearth deals are always solvable — but still losable

Here is the most important thing to understand. CardHearth builds every Mahjong deal by
**reverse-construction**: it lays the tiles down as pairs in a guaranteed-solvable
order, so a complete solution always exists. You will never be handed an impossible board. But
that does *not* mean you can remove pairs at random. If you take the wrong two tiles, you
can lock the remaining copies and reach a dead end on a board that was perfectly winnable. So
your entire strategy reduces to one idea: **don't get greedy**. Think before every
match.

## Five principles that clear the board

### 1. Before removing a pair, ask what it frees

Every match you make opens up the tiles that were touching it. Prefer the match that
**unlocks the most blocked tiles** — especially ones that were pinned on both
sides or buried underneath. A pair that frees nothing useful is a pair you usually want to
leave for later.

### 2. When a tile appears three or four times, choose carefully

With four copies of each tile in play, you will often see three or four of the same kind
available at once. This is the classic trap. Removing the **wrong two** can box in
the remaining copies so they can never be matched. Look at where all four sit, and remove the
pair that keeps the others reachable — or wait until it is clear which two are safe to take.

### 3. Work from the most blocking tiles downward

Tiles at the top of stacks and along the long **centre spine** pin huge numbers of
tiles beneath and beside them. Prioritise clearing those. Every tile you peel off the top or the
spine releases several others, where a match buried in a low corner frees almost nothing.

### 4. Keep matches in reserve

When you have more than one safe pair to take, you do not have to take them all immediately.
Holding a known match in reserve keeps your options open — it is a guaranteed move you can fall
back on while you work elsewhere. Flexibility is what prevents dead ends.

### 5. Use Hint and unlimited Undo

Because every deal is solvable, a dead end is never the board's fault — it means an earlier
removal closed off the path. On CardHearth, *Undo* is unlimited, so when you get stuck,
step back through your moves and try a different order rather than restarting. *Hint*
will point out an available match when you genuinely cannot find one.

## Common mistakes that lose games

- **Greedily clearing any available pair** — the number-one way to strand a
  solvable board.
- **Removing all four of a kind in the wrong order**, locking the last two.
- **Taking matches that free nothing useful** while blocking tiles stay buried.
- **Ignoring the centre spine**, which pins more tiles than anything else.
- **Forgetting undo exists** — explore a line and take it back if it dead-ends.

## Layouts: Turtle and beyond

The classic [**Turtle**](/mahjong-turtle/) layout — with its raised
centre spine and sloping shell — is the one most people picture, and it is a great place to
practise reading which tiles block which. CardHearth offers multiple layouts beyond the Turtle,
each with its own blocking patterns, so the same principles apply but the priorities shift. Once
you can spot the spine and the top-of-stack tiles on one layout, you will read the rest quickly.
