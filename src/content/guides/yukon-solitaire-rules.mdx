Yukon takes the familiar Klondike layout and removes the stockpile, then hands you a
much freer way to move cards. There is nothing to draw and nothing left to chance once the
down cards are turned: the whole deal is in front of you, and the puzzle is figuring out the
order to unpack it. This page is a complete reference to how Yukon is played — the deal, the
objective, and the one rule that makes it distinct. If you'd rather learn by doing, you can
[play Yukon](/yukon/) straight away.

## What is Yukon Solitaire?

Yukon is a solitaire game played with a single standard 52-card deck and just two kinds of
pile: **seven tableau columns** on the table and **four foundations**
that you build up to win. There is **no stock and no waste pile** — every card is
dealt into the columns at the start, so each move you make comes from what is already on the
table. That small change has a big effect: instead of waiting on the draw, you spend the whole
game rearranging what you can already see.

## The deal

All 52 cards go into the **seven tableau columns**. The first column gets a single
face-up card. Columns two through seven get **6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11 cards**
respectively. In each of those longer columns the cards at the bottom are dealt
**face down** and the **top five are turned face up**. That works out
to 1 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 9 + 10 + 11 = **52 cards**, the whole deck. The
**four foundations** start empty. Because five cards are showing on every long
column from the outset, you have plenty to work with on the very first move.

## The objective

You win by building all four **foundations** up by suit, each running from
**Ace to King**. Each foundation takes one suit: start it with that suit's Ace,
then add the Two, the Three and so on up to the King. When all four foundations hold a complete
Ace-to-King suit — 13 cards each, 52 in total — the game is won.

## Moving cards — Yukon's twist

On the tableau, columns are built **down in alternating colours**: a card lands on
a column only if it is one rank lower than the top card and the *opposite* colour — a
red Six on a black Seven, a black Four on a red Five. So far that is ordinary solitaire. Here
is what makes Yukon different:

**You may move any face-up card together with every card sitting on top of it — even if
that group is not in sequence.** Only the card you grab has to land legally. The cards
riding on top of it can be in any order, any suit, any colour. This is the rule that sets Yukon
apart from Klondike, where a group has to be a clean descending, alternating-colour run before
you can move it.

For example, suppose a column ends in a face-up red Eight, and stacked above that Eight is a
jumbled pile — say a black King, a red Two, a black Nine, in no particular order. If you have a
black Nine showing at the foot of another column, you can pick up that red Eight
*and the whole jumble on top of it* and drop the lot onto the black Nine, because the
Eight is one lower and the opposite colour. The cards above it just come along for the ride.
This lets you relocate big, untidy stacks to dig out the buried and face-down cards underneath
— the heart of Yukon strategy.

## Empty columns

When you clear a tableau column completely, it can only be filled by a **King** —
along with whatever cards happen to be stacked on top of that King. No other card may start an
empty column. Empty columns are precious for exactly this reason: they give you somewhere to
park a King-led group, which in turn frees up the cards that King was blocking.

## How you win

The game is won the moment every card sits on a foundation — all four suits built cleanly from
Ace to King, the tableau emptied out. Each time you move the cards above a face-down card, the
newly exposed card flips face up, so steady progress keeps turning over fresh material to work
with. Yukon is a game of skill, not luck: with no hidden stock and everything eventually in
view, strong play wins a large share of deals, and undo lets you back up and find a better line
when you get stuck.

## Yukon vs Klondike

Yukon and Klondike share the same tableau shape, but they play very differently. Klondike has a
stock and waste pile you draw from; Yukon has neither — every card is on the table from the
start. And where Klondike only lets you move a group if it is already a perfect descending,
alternating-colour run, Yukon lets you move *any* face-up card with everything piled on
top of it, in order or not. That single freedom is why Yukon feels so much more tactical: you
are constantly shifting messy stacks to expose what is hiding beneath them.
